What Is a Good Cap Rate for a Rental Property?

By Scott Pratt · Licensed Georgia Real Estate Broker · 16 years in the business

"Good" cap rate is one of the most misunderstood questions in real estate investing — because the honest answer is "it depends," and the things it depends on are exactly what separate a smart buy from an expensive mistake.

Ask ten investors what a good cap rate is and you'll get ten answers, most of them wrong in one direction or the other. Some chase the highest cap rate they can find and end up in properties that bleed them dry. Others dismiss anything under 8% and miss the safest deals in the best markets. After sixteen years in this business, I can tell you the cap rate matters — but only once you understand what it's actually telling you.

This guide breaks down what a cap rate is, how to calculate it, what range counts as "good," and — most importantly — when a low cap rate is the smarter buy and when a high one is a trap.

What a cap rate actually measures

The capitalization rate (cap rate) tells you the annual return a property produces based on its price, independent of financing. That last part is the key. Cap rate ignores your mortgage entirely — it answers the question, "If I paid all cash for this property, what percentage return would the income give me?"

That makes it a clean way to compare two properties on equal footing, because it strips out the distortions of who's putting how much down and at what interest rate. It's a measure of the property's earning power, not your particular loan.

Cap Rate = Net Operating Income (annual) ÷ Purchase Price

Net operating income (NOI) is the annual rent minus annual operating expenses — taxes, insurance, management, maintenance, vacancy — but before your mortgage payment. So a property with $24,000 in NOI bought at $300,000 has an 8% cap rate ($24,000 ÷ $300,000).

Broker's tip

Sellers and listing agents love to quote cap rates using rosy expense numbers that leave out vacancy, management, and real maintenance reserves. Always recalculate the cap rate yourself with honest expenses. A "9% cap" often becomes a 6% cap once you count what it actually costs to run the property.

So what's a good cap rate?

Here's the range most investors work within, and what each band usually signals:

Cap RateWhat it typically means
4% – 5%Strong, stable, appreciating markets. Lower income return, but often lower risk and better long-term growth.
6% – 8%The sweet spot for many buy-and-hold rentals — a healthy balance of cash flow and stability.
9% – 10%+Higher cash flow, but usually higher risk: weaker locations, older properties, or slower appreciation.

As a general rule, a cap rate between 5% and 10% is considered healthy for a residential rental. But that range is almost useless without context — and the context is everything.

Why a higher cap rate isn't automatically better

This is the mistake I see most often. New investors treat cap rate like a video-game score: higher must be better. It isn't.

A high cap rate means the market is demanding more return to compensate for more risk. That 10% cap property might be in a neighborhood with declining population, high tenant turnover, older systems that will need replacing, or little to no appreciation. You're getting paid more because you're taking on more.

Meanwhile, a 5% cap property in a growing, desirable area might deliver less monthly cash flow but appreciate steadily, attract better tenants, and hold its value through a downturn. Over a ten-year hold, the "lower" cap rate often wins.

The real question

Don't ask "is this cap rate high?" Ask "is this cap rate appropriate for the risk?" A 5% cap in a booming metro can be a great buy. A 5% cap in a declining rural town is overpriced. The number only means something next to the market.

Cap rate vs. cash-on-cash return

Cap rate and cash-on-cash return get confused constantly, so it's worth being clear. Cap rate measures the property's return based on price, ignoring your loan. Cash-on-cash return measures the return on the actual cash you put in, including financing.

Use cap rate to compare properties against each other and gauge whether a price is fair for the market. Use cash-on-cash to understand what your money is actually earning after leverage. Both matter, and a complete analysis looks at both — never just one.

How to use cap rate in a real decision

Here's how I actually put it to work when advising a buyer:

  • Recalculate it honestly — never trust the listing's number. Use real expenses including vacancy and reserves.
  • Compare it to the local market — pull cap rates on similar recently-sold rentals in the same area. That's your benchmark, not a national average.
  • Judge it against the risk — a below-market cap rate in a strong area can be worth it; an above-market cap rate in a weak one usually isn't the bargain it looks like.
  • Pair it with cash-on-cash — cap rate tells you if the price is fair; cash-on-cash tells you what your investment actually returns.

The bottom line

A good cap rate is one that's fair for the risk you're taking in the market you're buying in. For most residential rentals that lands somewhere between 5% and 10%, but the number alone never tells the story. A disciplined investor calculates it honestly, benchmarks it locally, weighs it against risk, and pairs it with cash-on-cash return before making a move.

Get those habits right and you'll pass on the "high cap rate" traps that catch less careful buyers — and recognize the strong, lower-cap deals that quietly build wealth over time.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good cap rate for a rental property?

For most rentals, 5% to 10% is considered healthy. Lower cap rates (4–6%) are common in strong, appreciating markets; higher ones (8–10%+) appear in higher-risk or slower-growth areas. What's "good" depends on the market and your goals.

How do you calculate cap rate?

Cap rate = net operating income ÷ purchase price. NOI is annual rental income minus annual operating expenses, before any mortgage payment.

Is a higher cap rate always better?

No. A higher cap rate means more return relative to price, but usually signals more risk — a weaker location or slower appreciation. A lower cap rate in a strong market can be the safer, smarter long-term play.

What's the difference between cap rate and cash-on-cash return?

Cap rate measures the property's return based on price, ignoring financing. Cash-on-cash measures the return on the actual cash you invested, including your loan. Cap rate compares properties; cash-on-cash measures your real yield.
